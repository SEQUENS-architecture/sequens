The morning board

Nine small things,
waiting downstairs.

Not one long task. A spread of quick questions, each one aimed at something your child actually needs, built before you came down. You read it out, or you put it on the wall.

Before you start

A morning board is not a lesson. It is the ten minutes that switches thinking on. Three of the nine are the same for everyone. The other six are aimed at one child, and no child is told why.

Board ready. All 9 built. Every cell generated and checked. A board is never shown half finished.

This morning's board

Nothing here is random. Turn this on to see why each question was chosen.

Ivy's morning board Tuesday · about 10 minutes

Why nine small things, and not one big one. A long task at eight in the morning asks a child to concentrate before they are ready. Nine short questions ask for one thought each, and each one finishes. Working memory is small, and a morning board should cost almost nothing to start.

Why these nine. Five fluency questions on the thing that was shaky, one on writing, one deliberately old, one that has no right answer, and two words. Turn on the working above and every cell tells you where it came from.

What your child never sees

Ivy is not on the easy board and Nell is not on the hard one. They are each on their own board, and the reason lives with you.

Going further is offered, never allocated. If a child finishes the board and wants more, the extra question is there for any of them. It is not attached to a name and it is not expected. Nobody is put on it.

When the board is done

Two minutes, three answers per child. That is the whole record, and it is what tomorrow's board is built from.

Yes, partly, or not yet. You were in the room, so you already know. Partly is a real answer and the most common one. A board that only offered yes and no would make you round up or round down, and tomorrow would be built on the rounding.

Gaps are normal. Every family has them and every school has them. A not yet is the most useful thing you can tell SEQUENS, because it is exactly what tomorrow aims at. Recording it honestly is worth more than recording it kindly.

What was sent, and what was not

Nothing about your child left your account. To build Ivy's five fluency questions, SEQUENS asked for five number bond questions to ten, for a five year old. That was the entire request. It did not send her name, her age in months, Monday's three wrong answers, or anything you have ever recorded about her.

The aiming is arithmetic, not a conversation. Choosing bonds to ten because Monday was three out of five is code, running on your own record, on your account. The model was only asked for the questions once the decision had already been made.

You see the board before your child does. It is built overnight, and it waits for you. Nothing is shown to a child that you have not looked at first.

Honest about what this is. This page is a demonstration built from a written specification. The board you are reading was written by hand to show the shape. Live generation, accounts and your own record are not built yet, and no board has been generated by the engine at the standard this shows.

And one honest cost. A board of nine aimed cells is nine separate generations, not one. That is slower and dearer than a single lesson, so fluency sets and word lists are reused rather than rebuilt each morning. It is named here because it has to be designed for, not discovered later.
